# Did Claude just prove the Riemann Hypothesis? A 167-year-old problem may be cracking

On November 20, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, published a statement on his personal website:

**He claims that, together with Claude, he has found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis — and that the method can be generalized further to the even broader Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH).**

This is no ordinary conjecture. Born in 1859, the Riemann Hypothesis has haunted mathematicians for 167 years. It is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's seven Millennium Prize Problems, carrying a $1 million reward.

Before judging the claim, let's first ask: **what does the Riemann Hypothesis actually say?**

## 1. What is the Riemann Hypothesis?

Primes are numbers divisible only by 1 and themselves, such as 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11.

They look lawless: sometimes clustered together, sometimes spaced far apart. Mathematicians have long wanted to know how the frequency of primes changes as numbers grow, and how to estimate how many primes fall within any given range.

In 1793, Gauss — only 16 years old — began working on the problem. He found that as numbers grow, primes appear less and less frequently. Mathematicians later found an elegant approximation formula for the number of primes not exceeding a given number.

But an approximation is still an approximation: a gap always remains between the estimate and the true count. Mathematicians naturally wanted to know how large that error really is, and whether it can be controlled more tightly.

In 1859, the German mathematician Riemann found a clever direction of attack: he converted the prime-counting problem into the study of a special function. He discovered that some special zeros of this function are tightly linked to the error in prime distribution. He then made a bold conjecture: all of these zeros should lie on one special line.

That is the famous Riemann Hypothesis. If it holds, mathematicians can describe the distribution of primes far more precisely.

## 2. How did Claude get involved?

Claude was not originally aiming to prove the Riemann Hypothesis.

In August 2026, a non-mathematician employee at Anthropic asked Claude to seriously take on the Riemann Hypothesis. It could not solve the problem outright, but it unexpectedly advanced a related question: proving that at least 67.2% of non-trivial zeros lie on the critical line described by the hypothesis, and that these zeros are simple zeros.

For context, the record previously held by mathematicians was only about 41.6%. In one step, Claude raised the known lower bound in this research direction by roughly 25 percentage points.

Anthropic's mathematicians then reviewed the proof and invited external mathematical experts to examine it. In September, the mathematician Youness Lamzouri posted a conceptually simpler proof of the same result on arXiv.

The experience convinced Anthropic that Claude was no longer just generating text that looked like mathematics — it could genuinely help mathematicians push a long-standing research question forward.

A few months later, Anthropic took aim at the Riemann Hypothesis itself.

## 3. Taking on the Riemann Hypothesis again

Fast-forward to November 20, 2026: Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge claims that he and Claude have found a complete proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, and that the method can even be extended to the harder Generalized Riemann Hypothesis.

The way it was released is rather unusual. Instead of publishing a traditional, readable mathematics paper, Alpöge released 14 compressed archives containing a large body of Lean formalized proof code.

Why not write a paper? By Alpöge's account, rewriting the proof in a human-readable form could run to more than 250,000 pages. Rather than publishing a paper almost no one could finish, he chose to release the formalized proof first, handing it to computers and other researchers for inspection.

## 4. Has the Riemann Hypothesis really been proven?

The claim quickly drew the mathematics community's attention, and Fields Medalist Terence Tao also noted the development on his blog.

He did not endorse the idea that the hypothesis has been conquered. Instead, he pointed out that a proof of this scale still needs further verification and digestion — in particular, mathematicians need to understand the proof strategy inside it.

After all, Lean can rigorously check mathematical reasoning, but only if the code genuinely expresses the theorem we intend to prove, with no hidden assumptions or other defects.

By publishing the formalized proof code, Alpöge has indeed opened the door to independent verification. But as of now, the proof has not completed independent review by the mathematics community, and no formal paper that a human can read in full exists.

So the more accurate description is this: an Anthropic mathematician and Claude claim to have found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, released as Lean formalized code, and the mathematics community is now verifying the result.

Whatever the final outcome, the attempt is already astonishing. If the proof is eventually confirmed, it will not only be a major milestone for AI in mathematics research — it will mark AI moving from assisting mathematicians with problems to independently exploring and discovering mathematical structure.
